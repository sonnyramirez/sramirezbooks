Chapter 1: Archie’s Childhood

Archie came into the world unwanted and unloved. Someone put him into a cardboard box and left him in the rain behind the old fish market, wrapped in a thin rag that never stood a chance against the weather. Rain hammered the alley hard enough to wash grease, blood, and fish scales toward the gutter, and by morning the box had gone soft at the corners and started folding in on itself. The rag clung to him, and the stink of saltwater and rotten fish worked its way into his fur before he was old enough to understand what cold meant. Whoever dropped him there never came back.

A city worker found him at first light while loading junk into the back of a rusted municipal van. Archie got tossed in with the rest of the trash headed for the dump. He might have ended up there too if somebody hadn’t noticed a little patch of orange fur sticking out from the heap. One of the workers pulled him free and called it in. The boss took one look at him and sent him to the city orphanage instead.

That place was a three-story brick building with cracked windows, warped floors, and a smell that never quite left your nose once it got in there. It was supposed to be a shelter for kittens and puppies. It ran more like a training ground for predators, and a kitten either got tough in there or got eaten alive.

Every meal turned into a test. The first few mornings, Archie learned how to eat fast. The moment he looked away to grab his cup, another paw came across his tray. Sometimes it was a piece of bread, or half the meal. After a while, Archie quit wondering if somebody was going to take his food and started wondering who was going to try. The bigger orphans ruled the hallways, and the staff let them, as long as no blood showed up where visitors could see it.

Archie learned the rules early because that place respected nothing but instinct and nerve. There were animals who took, and there were animals who got stripped down until there was nothing worth taking anymore. He made up his mind young that he was never going to be the second kind. By the time he was eight, animals in the mess hall already knew who he was. Still, there were always bigger ones who thought size was what mattered.

One night, a tabby almost twice his size leaned across the table and tried to snatch the last dinner roll off Archie’s tray. He probably thought the little orange kitten would yelp for help, maybe cry, and let size decide the outcome. Archie came out of his seat so fast the bench scraped backward. His hook landed square on the tabby’s jaw and sent him sideways across a table full of startled animals and overturned trays.

The room went quiet for a second. The staff glanced over, saw exactly what had happened, and went right back to eating. After that, the older cats started paying attention.

At first it was little stuff. He’d slip a note under somebody’s door, or keep a staff member busy for ten seconds while one of the older cats walked off with extra bread. Archie did it because work, even dirty work, had value. Every errand brought him a little closer to the scarred cats with contraband under their mattresses, and every favor made the place a little easier to live in.

By ten, he had figured out how to get out through the laundry chute. He started slipping out at night and hitting the streets when the city was careless enough to let him. He’d grab a loaf from a bakery, or fish off a docked trawler if the watch animal was half-asleep. Once in a while, if luck lined up with nerve, he came back with a whole sack of smoked mackerel tucked under his arm and rain dripping from his whiskers.
