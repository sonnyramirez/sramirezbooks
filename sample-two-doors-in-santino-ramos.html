Chapter 1: Santino Ramos

Santino Ramos grew up in a neighborhood where people knew how to mind their own business. Most houses on his block needed paint or some kind of repair. Fences leaned with mismatched planks. On most weekends, men worked under car hoods while younger kids cut through yards that didn’t belong to them. Music came through open windows with the smell of tortillas and chiles roasting on the stove. Exhaust fumes and hot asphalt lingered in the air. Kids played in the street until names started getting called from front porches. Everybody knew which parents gave warnings and which parents came outside.

Santino was the youngest of three boys, always following his brothers wherever they let him. Lorenzo, the oldest, and Diego, the middle brother, moved through the neighborhood with a confidence Santino envied but hadn’t earned. They knew which blocks were safe to cross, which older guys only talked tough, which neighbors told parents everything, and which situations meant trouble was close enough to notice.

Their father, Lazaro Ramos, believed boys needed discipline before the street got hold of them. He trained all three sons in Goju-Kai karate in the backyard, on cracked concrete, old mats, and the kind of patience a man needed if he wanted to teach his boys right. He corrected their feet, their breathing, their hands, and the way they carried themselves. When Santino lost focus, Lazaro made him start again. When he got angry, Lazaro made him start again slower.

“Again,” Lazaro would say.

Santino would drop his hands. “I did it already.”

“You moved,” Lazaro said. “That ain’t the same as doing it right.”

Lorenzo and Diego knew better than to laugh when Lazaro was correcting him, but Santino could feel them wanting to. Lazaro saw that too.

“All three of you,” he said. “From the beginning.”

That was how Lazaro taught. One boy lost focus, all three started over. Lazaro wasn’t trying to be cruel. He wanted them to understand that one man’s mistake could cost everybody he was with.

Lorenzo and Diego also boxed. They trained at a small gym that smelled like leather, sweat, old towels, and grown men who had already taken their lessons the hard way. Santino was too young to train, so he stayed on the sidelines. In that gym, anger got answered with a bell, a coach, and another man’s gloves. Power in the hands meant little if the feet were wrong.

Lorenzo took to both worlds better than anybody expected. He had size without showing off and confidence without treating people beneath him. Adults talked about him as if a future was already waiting. He ran in the mornings before curtains opened, trained after school, helped around the house, and spoke about leaving one day like he had already made a promise to himself. Santino believed effort was supposed to mean something because Lorenzo made it look that way.

One afternoon, Santino sat at the kitchen table with his schoolwork spread in front of him, pencil in hand, frustration working its way into his face. The numbers on the page kept turning into something he couldn’t grab. Every wrong answer made him press harder until the pencil point snapped.

Lorenzo came in through the back door with his gym bag over one shoulder and sweat soaked through his shirt. He glanced at the broken pencil, then at Santino.

“You fighting the homework now?”

Santino pushed the paper away. “This is stupid. I don’t know what they want me to do.”

Lorenzo dropped his bag near the wall and came to the table. He didn’t snatch the paper or make Santino feel dumb for struggling. He leaned over his little brother’s shoulder and studied the problem.

“Slow down,” Lorenzo said. “You keep rushing, you’re going to miss the part that tells you what to do.”

“I’ve been looking at it.”

“You’ve been getting mad at it. That ain’t the same thing.”

Santino raised his face. “You think you know because you’re older?”

Lorenzo smiled a little. “I know because I already took this same math course.”

He showed Santino where one number had to move and where the next step started. Once Santino saw it, the problem made more sense than he wanted to admit. Lorenzo tapped the paper with two fingers.

“See? You had it. You just got mad too fast.”

Santino picked up the broken pencil piece and gave him a look. “You’re pretty smart, bro. I didn’t know a punching bag had brains.”

Lorenzo liked that one, but he wasn’t about to let his little brother get away with it. “And I didn’t know a little knucklehead could do math, so I guess we both learned something.”

“I’m not a knucklehead,” Santino said.

“You snapped a pencil over math.”

“That doesn’t make me a knucklehead.”

“It doesn’t help your case either.”

Santino tried to stay serious, but the corner of his mouth gave him away. Lorenzo smiled first, which made Santino feel like he had won something even though he had barely finished one problem.

“Lorenzo,” Santino said, turning back to the paper, “do you know what you want to be when you get older?”

Lorenzo leaned against the table and thought for a second. “I don’t know. Maybe box. Maybe open a gym one day. Teach kids how to box. Keep them out of trouble if I can.”

“That might work,” Santino said. “But you gotta learn how to box first.”

Lorenzo’s face changed. “What did you say?”

Santino was already laughing when Lorenzo grabbed him around the neck and pulled him from the chair. They stumbled away from the table, Santino twisting to get loose while Lorenzo held him easy, both of them laughing too loud for the kitchen.

Gloria turned from the stove with a spoon in her hand. “Lorenzo, if he breaks that chair, you’re fixing it. Santino, finish that homework before you two turn my kitchen into a gym.”

Lorenzo let him go only after Santino promised he had learned the math. That was Lorenzo to Santino then: gym bag by the wall, sweat on his shirt, homework spread across the table, and enough patience to help his little brother before clowning him for needing help in the first place. Lorenzo trained hard, helped at home, listened to Gloria, teased Santino, and talked about a future like a man could earn one if he stayed disciplined enough. To Santino, Lorenzo made that belief feel possible.

On the warm Friday night of June 17, 1983, Lorenzo walked home from his girlfriend’s house and never made it home. He had made that walk plenty of times. Four or five blocks. Same sidewalks, same porch lights, same turns. Nothing about the route should have mattered. A young man should have been able to walk home from seeing his girlfriend without it becoming the last thing he ever did.
