Chapter 1: The Cat In The Velvet Townhouse

The Randall townhouse stood on a Cambridge street where the lamps glowed gold at dusk and the cobblestones retained the scent of rain long after the clouds had passed. The brass door fittings were always polished, the white curtains hung straight and measured to the inch, and pots of lavender filled the window boxes, trimmed, watered, and aligned like sentries.

Inside, the house displayed the same devotion to order. The air carried a constant trace of Earl Grey, wax, and old books. The oak floors were polished daily until the crystal chandelier appeared to double its light in their reflection. Bookshelves climbed from floor to cornice, packed with leather-bound volumes and old atlases pressed tightly together. If anything was left crooked, someone corrected it before the day was over.

His father, Professor Edmund Randall, believed chaos was a failure of preparation and that a great deal of trouble could be traced back to an animal not thinking far enough ahead.

“Mister Eric,” he would say in his even voice, “what flaw brought down the Roman Empire?”

Sometimes Mister Eric answered while chewing. “Internal division. Rome became too unstable, corrupt, and divided to defend itself effectively.”

Professor Randall would continue whenever Mister Eric answered correctly. “How many dynasties ruled ancient Egypt?”

“Thirty,” Mister Eric said, with rather more confidence than the question warranted.

“Mister Eric, we covered this only recently. The answer is thirty-one. You were wrong not because you did not know it, but because you failed to consider what you already knew. The dynasties extended from 3100 BCE to 332 BCE. The figures were in your head, yet you did not make proper use of them. A careless answer may have consequences that last a lifetime. Do remember that, my boy.”

His mother, Lucinda Randall, handled evenings in her own fashion. She had a softer voice than Edmund and a warmer way of teaching, though she expected just as much. She sat by Mister Eric’s bed with pawwritten cards in neat stacks, each card carrying a word or phrase from another language.

“Bonsoir, mon petit.”

“Bonsoir, maman.”

If he stumbled, she corrected him with patience and made him try again. If he got it right, she gave a small nod and moved on. She never drowned him in praise. She wanted the work to matter more than the compliment. She would talk to him in English, then suddenly change to either French, Spanish, or Russian, without letting him know that’s what they were doing, and he would answer her back in the language she chose.

By ten, he could greet a guest in French, Spanish, and a careful amount of Russian that still needed work. Lucinda taught him more than sound. She explained where words came from, how meaning changed when power changed paws, how trade, conquest, faith, and hunger all left their mark on speech.

“Pay attention to words,” she told him once. “They are the gateway to the animals.” He always remembered that.

The winter he turned eleven, the wireless in his father’s study stopped working. It was a walnut cabinet with a cloth front and two brass dials, and Edmund listened to it every evening at six for the news and again on Sunday afternoons for the concert from London. For three evenings it gave out nothing but a low hum. On the fourth, Mister Eric asked whether he might try to mend it.

Edmund considered the request for longer than Mister Eric expected. Then he spread a fresh linen cloth over the study floor, set the wireless on it, and put a small screwdriver in his son’s paw.

“You may open it,” he said, “on one condition. Every part that comes out is to be laid on this cloth in a line, in the order it came out, and nothing is to be moved once it has been put down.”

“Why must it be in order?”

“Because the order in which a thing comes apart is the order in which it goes back together, only reversed. The last part you remove is the first part you replace. Keep to that, and you will never find yourself holding a screw with no notion of where it belongs.”
